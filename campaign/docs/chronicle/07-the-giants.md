---
title: The Giants
part: Beyond the dyke, spring 465 · played 8 May 2026
---

West of the border town there are no roads to speak of: hills, forest, wild men and the odd poor village,
the empty country between the great kingdoms. Merlin rode at the back and sulked. The knights were in good
spirits. The Easter feast had been a good one, and they still hoped to do both errands and be home by
Pentecost.

Asked about dragons, Sir Elad laughed, which he rarely does, and said his squire was the most sensible one
of them: a man who meets a worm should come back with an army. There are a great many more dragon lunches
than dragon slayers. Sir Eamon said they were more likely to meet giants in these parts; the Cambrians have
had trouble with them for as long as anyone remembers. Sir Hywel wondered aloud what dragon tasted like, and
Sir Moriad told him he would tilt at anything, even a windmill.

They came to what looked like a range of overgrown hills and was in fact a dyke: the old border of Cambria,
built by one of the Briton kings against the Romans, for all the good it did him. Sineda saw that they were
not the first riders this way. A dozen or more mounted men had passed some days before, and the knights put
him at the front to keep them on the trail. He and Drust kept it all day. Among the hoofprints were the
tracks of men on foot, running uphill, and in places the horses had trodden on the footprints: the riders
had been chasing the men on foot. Paun wondered if it was a feint, to draw them into ground where horses were
useless. Sir Eamon said that was very like the Cambrians, and they closed up.

Drust saw a glint of metal in a valley between two ridges, a fine place for an ambush. The knights rode down
first while the squires watched from the ridges, then whistled them in. They had missed the fight. There was
a mass grave, hastily dug, and a sword stood over it; that was what had caught the light. There were drag
marks leading away uphill. There were no horses in the grave, and none anywhere, and no sign that they had
run. Sir Eamon showed the boys a bare footprint in soft earth under a branch, as big as any of their heads.
A giant, and it had very likely eaten the horses.

Paun prayed for the dead, since there was no priest, and said the last rites as he had learned them by rote,
in Latin he did not understand, and the Christian knights joined him. Whoever had buried these men had taken
their gear. The trail of whoever had done the burying led away north-west; the giant's footprints and the drag
marks led east. The squires argued for the drag marks, since every hour lost made it less likely they would
find Roderick alive, and the knights agreed.

## The caves

The marks went uphill to three caves in the hillside, with a midden of bones to one side and footprints of
great size coming out of all three. The knights conferred. Paun had never thought of giants as coming more
than one at a time. There might be a family. They would not fight giants at night, when giants can see and
men cannot, and the stories said these came down after dark to raid the villages; so they would go in now,
while the giants slept. The knights split into three pairs, one pair to a cave, and took the squires with
them. Sir Elad sent Sineda with Sir Eamon, Lord Clefford, Paun and Drust. Lothwellen went with Sir Elad and
Sir Hywel. Caradoc and Gorthyn stayed with the horses.

Inside it stank. Paun and Sineda heard snoring, two kinds, from two directions, and Paun held up two
fingers. There was a table sized for giants, piled with gnawed bones, and hanging in a corner, human limbs
and pieces of horse, and a smell of meat smoking, as though someone were laying in for winter in the spring.
In a corner, Sineda saw two men tied up: a young man and a knight. Lord Clefford said, under his breath, that
it was Squire Roderick, and that must be his knight. Get them out, the knights said. We will deal with what is
in here.

Something barked outside, and something inside answered. A great hound with red eyes rose from beside the
fire. Paun loosed an arrow at it in the dark and missed. It went for Lord Clefford, who cut it; Drust stabbed
at it and missed; Paun brought his great mace down on its skull, and it was dead. Then they heard the giant
whose dog it was.

Sineda cut Roderick and Sir Perrin down. Roderick asked if they had brought help; Sir Perrin had taken a club
to the head and was insensible, and Roderick, his hands still tied, got an arm under him and dragged him
toward the light. The first giant came out of the dark at Sineda, and Sineda, who hates giants, drew the
jewelled sword of his family and opened its flesh. It reached him in two strides. A second came, a female,
and Lord Clefford, who never refuses a charge, vaulted the table at her. Sir Eamon hesitated, and then did
not; Drust stepped in beside Sineda and took a blow that rang his head. The giant clubbed Sineda off his feet.
Lord Clefford opened the female's belly, and Sir Eamon ducked round behind her and drove his sword into her
back up to the hilt, and she fell. Paun, who had stayed to make sure his fellows got out, ran back and swung
his mace into the first giant so hard that it reeled and could not get away, and went down, and Drust
finished it with his spear.

The other caves had been worse. Sir Moriad and Sir Rayne had fought three giants in theirs, and Sir Elad, Sir
Hywel and Lothwellen two more. A whole clan had lived in these hills. Only the squires' cave held anyone
alive. Paun went through every cave to make sure every giant was dead. They found a heap of weapons and armour
in a corner, taken from the dead.

When he could speak, Roderick told them what had happened. Sir Perrin's company had followed Cambrian raiders
over the dyke into an ambush. They had been holding their own until the giants came down on all of them, and
then everyone turned on the giants, and the giants killed most of them and took the horses. Sir Perrin had
not been in his right mind for days.

They rode back toward Cameliard with Sir Perrin and Roderick on the squires' horses, since theirs were all
eaten. Paun had begun drafting verses about the Wolves in the cave, to the tune of his Lay of Squire's Hill,
and they were not yet good; his fellows heard some of them. Merlin was not pleased. He understood that
Roderick had to be rescued, but the banner had to reach its bearer before winter, he said, and every delay
on other business brought winter nearer. He only knew roughly where it lay himself.
