---
title: The Fathers
part: The squires and their families, 439–464 · made at the table, 14 November and 5 December 2025
---

Knighthood is new in Logres. The kings are still working out what it is: that a lord hands his sons to
another man to be raised, that a boy serves as a page and then a squire and is then made a knight. It is
the second generation to go through it. The squires of this campaign were born into it, and into
Vortigern's reign.

## Where they come from

Paun and Gorthyn are Cymric boys of the Belgae, the tribe whose lands became the county of Salisbury, and
their families knew each other. Sineda and Drust are Picts of Jagent in Cornwall, cousins, whose people
came south from Caledonia a generation ago on Dux Vortigern's promise of land. Lothwellen is of the
Catuvellauni, from the Saxon Shore in the east, where the invaders land first. Caradoc is a Cambrian from
Powys. Paun and Sineda are Christians. Gorthyn, Drust, Lothwellen and Caradoc keep the old gods.

## Sir Geraint ap Llwyd

Paun's father fought the Irish at the River Parrot in 440 and came home with a scar and one eye. He
stood at the coronation of Constans. He fought the Picts at Lincoln, dead eye and all, and did so well
that it was the making of his name. He kept his head down while Vortigern gave the Saxons land, and
while the complaints grew. Hunting in the woodlands in 450 he offended the fair folk, who cursed his
family's hunting; Paun carries the curse still. In 454 he fought the Irish in Cornwall.

In 456 he turned dissident and fell ill. In 457 he fought for the rebels at the Battle of Kent, and died
on that field. Paun's uncle took the lands in stewardship. Part of the family went over the sea to
Brittany. A cousin fought at Watsom Channel, kin fought at Ebbsfleet, and at the Night of the Long Knives
the Saxons killed Paun's uncle.

## Gorthyn's father

He fought the Irish at Carlion, and at Constans's coronation he held the court with a story of a fair
woman he had met in the forest, told so well that people were never sure it had not happened. Some
began to wonder whether there was fairy blood in the family. He complained to the king in 449, and the
king sent him to fight the Huns at Châlons, which he survived. He raided the Irish, fought for Vortimer
at Aylesford, and was one of the heroes of the Battle of Kent even though it was lost. He fought at
Watsom Channel and at Ebbsfleet. At the Night of the Long Knives he was seated at the king's table, and
did not come out.

## Sineda's family

Sineda's grandfather was one of the Pictish bodyguards set to protect King Constans. When Constans was
murdered, Vortigern had every one of them beheaded, and the Picts of Jagent have believed ever since that
they were framed. Sineda's father kept a decorated weapon he had been given by Vortigern's own hand. He
turned dissident, fought at Lincoln on the side against the king, went to Châlons, raided the Irish, was
sold out to them by a family friend, cleared a nest of water leapers from the family's lands and helped
drive off a giant. He joined Vortimer early and recruited for him. He was at Ebbsfleet beside Vortimer
when the prince took his death wound, and heard his last words: to be buried on the coast facing Thanet,
so that the Saxons would never settle there. The prince was buried in Colchester instead. Sineda's father
died at the Night of the Long Knives, seated next to Gorthyn's.

## Lothwellen's father

Lothwellen's father kept quiet for years. He fought at Lincoln on the winning side and came home with a
jewelled sword taken from a Pict. He was at Vortigern's wedding to Rowena, because he had never
complained. Then, in 456, he joined the rebellion anyway. He fought beside Catigern at Aylesford and saw
him and Horsa kill each other; he fought at the Battle of Kent, at Stonar and at Ebbsfleet, and ran
errands for Vortimer between. He died at the Night of the Long Knives.

## 464

Vortigern himself was taken by the Saxons that night, and ransomed, and he has gone into hiding. Saxon
war bands took London, Dorchester and Silchester and plundered where they liked. At Sarum the squires,
still pages, held the walls with their knights against Saxon pillagers. To the boys who had grown up on
their fathers' stories, it looked as though their fathers had been right about the Saxons.
