When the feast wound down the squires gathered on the floor while the Wolves sat at the high table with
Rhodri, who looked to be of an age with them. Sir Hywel came down with a jug of wine for the boys. They
were trying to learn from their host what had become of Roderick, he said, and he wondered whether Merlin
would take it badly if they turned aside from the banner to go looking. The boy was their lord, the heir
of Salisbury, and he had to be found and put back in his seat.

Sineda thought the brothers' quarrel was none of theirs, except that whoever lost it would blame the Wolves
and hold it against Roderick. Paun, who had taken more of the wine than he meant to and discovered too late
where his limit was, asked why, if Merlin's dreams were so special, he did not simply say which brother
would win, and whether Merlin might be one of the fair folk. Sir Hywel told him not to say that too loudly
around Lord Clefford.

So Sir Hywel sent them out. Sineda took a jug to the other squires, who gossiped as squires do. Roderick's
knight was Sir Perrin, they said, and it had gone badly with Sir Perrin's last squire, who was taken by the
Saxons, and who, the rumour went, was boiled alive instead of ransomed. A fortnight ago Sir Perrin and his
company had gone west to deal with raids across the Cambrian border, and nothing had been heard since. They
wanted to know whether the Wolves would help their lord against his brother. Sineda told them that was not
the Wolves' business, and that if it came to it he hoped they would be on the same side.

Drust went looking for the castellan, Barnabas, a pious man, and found him at the front of the midnight mass.
He sat down beside him and stayed awake through a long sermon in Latin. Afterwards Barnabas asked why he had
not taken communion; Drust said he did not feel himself worthy, and Barnabas told him that none of us are,
and that he should be baptised. Then he told Drust what he knew: Sir Perrin and his men had been sent west to
the border against Cambrian raiders, and there had been no word of them in a fortnight, and no one to spare
to find out. Perhaps questing knights might look into it.

Paun went looking for Merlin, on his hands and knees, looking under mushrooms, and found a pair of feet in the
courtyard in the moonlight. Merlin was not at the feast. He was not one of the fair folk, he said. Which
brother would win? Neither: they would kill each other, and one of their cousins would rule. The squires
should fight for Britain, against the tyrant, and to do it they must find a banner to fight under. Once the
banner was found, the man would reveal himself. Paun was sick over a railing. Merlin told him to keep away
from drink, and asked what kind of life he wanted for himself; Paun did not presume to have one he liked.
He went in, full of Merlin's certainty, to tell Sir Eamon that the princes were wasting the Wolves' time, and
Sir Eamon heard him out and said he would do what he could to steer the others.

Paun set off to find Drust and did not. He found a comfortable bed instead and fell asleep in it. He woke to
the touch of a lady with golden hair bound up as married women wear it, in a blue dress sewn with pearls,
whom he had seen at the high table. She asked how he came to be in her bed, and whether he would like to stay.
He did not understand what was happening until she kissed him, and then he got out of the bed and out of the
room as courteously as a boy in a panic can, and ran. He never learned her name.

Drust took what he had learned to Lord Clefford. It does not take two weeks to go to Cambria and back, Lord
Clefford said. Something had happened.

## West

The Wolves were up early. They rode west out of Cameliard. When the road forked north, Merlin turned north,
and the knights did not. They were going to find out what had become of Sir Perrin, Sir Elad said. Merlin said
it was a mistake. He had hoped they would not choose this. It meant only that the future would arrive later
than he had thought, he told Drust, and he would say no more.

After some days they came to a fortified town at the border, built like the old Roman forts, four-square
with four gates and no hill to stand on. The gates were shut. The watch asked which king of Cameliard they served,
and the answer that came back was "Both." None of the knights contradicted it, and after a moment's
confusion the gates opened. Sir Perrin and his company had ridden out the day after Sunday last, the townsfolk said, to deal with the
Cambrian raiders who had been taking their sheep and cattle all winter, and had not come back. Now the raids had
stopped. Beyond the dyke the country was mountains, and a horse would be little use there.

Drust heard the peasants say there were giants in the hills too, a family of them, and that whole villages'
livestock had gone to feed them. They looked at Sir Rayne as he went by, and worried for their cattle.
