title: Midsummer at Sarum
part: A summer's day in 462 · played 19 December 2025
---

It was a summer day at Sarum, three years before the story proper, and they all remember it. The pages
had been told they would train with the squires that day, in the drill yard at first light.

Someone had slipped them a bottle of wine the night before, and they had passed it round until it was
gone. Paun and Gorthyn were up with the dawn and properly dressed. Drust and Lothwellen came late and
half-buckled. Sineda was still asleep, until a bald, scowling knight hauled him out of bed by the ear and
paraded him round the yard in his small clothes. The pages learned later that this was Sir Elad of Vagon.
The lesson was that when he says dawn, he means dawn.

## The drill yard

Sir Elad put Sineda, unarmed and unarmoured, against Gorthyn, who was dressed, equipped, and much the
bigger boy. Sineda asked for a stick instead of a sword and was told no. Everyone he fights is bigger and
stronger than he is, and he has learned what they leave open when they think a small boy will be easy.
Gorthyn, proud and sure of himself, left it open. Sineda put him on the ground.

Drust and Lothwellen were close, blow for blow, until Drust found an opening low and took it; they
clasped hands after. Gorthyn, beaten once and not about to be beaten twice, was set against Paun, who meant to fight carefully.
Gorthyn charged him head down, swinging, and came up under his guard. Then Sineda and Drust were set
against each other, and neither did well.

In another field stood the newest thing from the continent: a quintain, a shield on a pivot with a
weight on the other arm that swings round and strikes a rider who hits it too slowly. They rode at it with
stirrups, also new. Lothwellen kept his speed and went straight through. Paun, quiet and set on doing it
well, was a little slow, and the weight clouted him on the back of the head; he kept his seat and his
composure, just. Gorthyn went through cleanly. Drust, whom horses do not like, was clouted too. Sineda
does not like horses either. He crossed himself, set his lance, and bounced down the lane with the lance
tip wobbling, and hit the shield so squarely that the weight passed over his head. The watching knights
applauded. Sir Elad allowed that perhaps he had needed the extra sleep.

They knew by then that some of the knights watching were not usually there: a company of six who were
always together, the Wolves of Vagon.

## The feast

That evening the Wolves came back from hunting with a boar, and there was a small feast. The pages served
it and ate on the floor, below the salt, with the dogs.

Drust fell into conversation with a young lady of good family, and kept her attention for the whole feast.
At the end of it he asked the Earl of Salisbury's leave to court her, and was told, kindly, to come back when
he was a knight with land.

Someone passed the pages a jug of strong wine. Lothwellen drank and called for more; Gorthyn, handed it
second time round, emptied it. Paun took a sip, to be polite, and passed it on. He meant to make a good
impression, and he played the harp and sang. Later he found himself making polite, ignorant conversation
about falconry with Sir Hywel, who loves it. Then Branwen, who had been chosen to lead the next dance, chose
Paun. He did not know what to do with the attention. He stepped on her toes, apologised, and stepped on
them again, and she laughed at him good-naturedly, with a smile he could not read.

Sineda had been low all day. With the wine in him he tried his luck with the prettiest of the serving
girls, and it worked. He left the feast with her and did not come back. The others worked his tables as
well as their own so that no one would miss him.

Gorthyn, drunk, decided to impress one of the knights, and chose the biggest: Sir Rayne of Wylye, the only
one not wearing a cross. He spilled on him, ate off his plate, took the plate before he had finished, and
did not stop talking. Sir Rayne told him, no more wine for you, and let it go.

Lothwellen was the most genial of them all. He took the knights' praise for his promise modestly, and
spent a round of the feast helping a lost child find its parents.

None of them noticed, until afterwards, that the Wolves had been watching the pages all evening. The wine
had come from one of them.
