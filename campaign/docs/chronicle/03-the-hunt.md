title: The Hunt
part: The next two days, 462 · played 2 January 2026

The morning after the feast, Paun was first into the drill yard, before most of the older squires. He was
not the first there. Sir Rayne of Wylye was already out, beating a straw dummy the size of a man with a
sword most of the pages could not have lifted in two hands. Paun offered to help, and was sent for a
pitcher of water. On the way to the kitchens he stumbled over Sineda, asleep in a corner with the serving
girl from the night before, and woke him with a couple of kicks and no explanation.

Sir Rayne drank half the water and poured the rest over his head, and asked Paun what he wanted to learn.
Paun said he did not understand how battle really goes, from inside it. Sir Rayne told him the secret was
a cool head: when men are screaming and your horse is going down, you think only of the next thing to be
done, and then the thing after. Paun said he knew the feeling. When a boy had fallen from a wall and cracked
his head open, everything had gone clear, and the fear had been somewhere far off.

Caradoc joined them there. He was a Cambrian from Powys whose family had lost its lands in the rebellion;
he had been a page at Sarum as long as the others, and had missed the day before, ill in the priests' care.
Lord Clefford arrived next, asking what lies Sir Rayne was putting into the boys' heads, and the two of
them argued happily about which of them was always pulling the other out of the scrum. Clefford had
brought down an Irish chieftain the season before and been paid his ransom.

Paun and Caradoc were set to spar with weighted wooden swords, the first real training swords either had
held. Paun used his height and pressed from above until Caradoc's parry was late, and put him down. Sir
Rayne helped Caradoc up and showed him where his form had failed. Then Sineda turned up, and faced Caradoc,
and wanted a stick instead of a sword, and was still looking over his shoulder about it when the signal
came; he stepped aside at the last moment and dropped his sword on Caradoc. Lothwellen, late and very
unwell, chose that moment to charge in from behind with a stick, screaming, to show that an attack can come
at any time. He tripped. The two of them ended in a heap. Sir Elad said that was quite true, a battle can
happen at any time, especially when a man is drunk.

## The armoury

Drust was ill, and stayed behind. The Wolves took the other four somewhere pages were never allowed: the
armoury, racks of spears and maces and axes, bows on the walls, and gaps along one wall where the swords
should have been. They were to go hunting, and should be armed for it. Paun wanted a great mace and could
not find one, so took the biggest club there was. Caradoc took a small throwing axe. Sineda took a
two-handed spear, the nearest thing to a stick he was offered. Lothwellen took a bow and, because someone
reminded him, arrows. They were fitted for padded gambesons and given old pot helms, and chose their
horses. Only Caradoc chose well: a light, quick rouncey.

On the ride out, Paun rode beside Sir Hywel and asked him about falconry. He had been thinking: if his family
could not hunt, perhaps a bird could hunt for them. Sir Hywel told him how he had raised his own bird from
the egg, and that Paun could begin with a bought one once he had land to keep it on. Lord Clefford said he
would never catch anything bigger than a mouse. Sineda held the reins wrapped round his arms and the mane
in both hands and watched the ground. Caradoc asked Sir Eamon for news of Cambria. Sir Eamon, who had once
served the King of Estregales and crossed swords with Cymric knights, had none, but he asked Caradoc
carefully about a letter Caradoc had received from a friend abroad, and about the rebel prince. Caradoc, who
looked up to him, said he had heard nothing lately. Lothwellen was sick over the side of his horse, and then rode up to apologise to
Sir Elad for the morning. Sir Elad said he would take up the idea of an ambush drill, and that Lothwellen
would be among the first ambushed.

## The forester

At the woods a forester met them with a scent hound and a speech in thick Gaelic, a list of what they might
hunt. It was Sir Eamon's trick: the boys were to choose their quarry without understanding the choice. But
Sineda can read and write Gaelic, and he understood every word, and said so. Paun understood most of it and
kept that to himself. They chose deer.

Lothwellen swore on his honour to bring down the finest buck in the forest, and led the hunt. The hounds
cornered a doe against a copse, and he loosed and missed, and she was gone. He kept after her until dark,
long after the others had given up, and walked out of the forest at last to find the Wolves at a camp with
a boar roasting. They had meant to take a boar all along; their horses could run one down. Sir Moriad was
favouring one leg. Sir Hywel's falcon had brought him a rabbit.

Over supper the knights talked about hunting. For deer, a bow or a javelin; for boar, a long spear, to keep
it off you; for a bear, do not. Paun sat by Sir Eamon, who played the harp and told story after story, all of
them about other men, and none about himself. Sir Eamon asked Paun what becoming a knight meant to him.
Paun said he wanted to inherit, and to live up to his father's name. Then he tried, clumsily, to say that
sometimes one serves best by not serving the man on the throne. Sir Eamon stopped him before he said too
much. These were dangerous times, he said. Keep your own counsel.

Sineda sat by Sir Elad, the one quiet man among the Wolves, and they shared a comfortable silence.
Lothwellen got up a dice game with Lord Clefford and Sir Hywel, kept them talking, and won the pot. Caradoc
asked Sir Rayne about his gods. Sir Rayne told him that the bear goddess Artio had protected him when he was
a boy, and that he meant to raise her a shrine one day in the woods at Wylye; he told Caradoc where the
shrine to Caradoc's own war god stood near Sarum, and how to get through a Christian mass. From all of it
the boys gathered that the Wolves had little love for what Vortigern was doing.

## The boar

The next day they went after deer again. Lord Clefford told them to bring back dinner or go hungry. Caradoc
led, and fell, and got up and led again, and the hounds found something. It was not a deer. It was a
full-grown boar, cornered and furious.

Sineda jumped down with his two-handed spear and, on his honour, drove it in. The boar stayed on its feet.
When it came for him he met it with the spear and missed, and it threw him across the clearing, and he did
not get up. Caradoc rode it down on his rouncey and wounded it. Lothwellen came in with his spear on his
honour and put it through the boar, and the boar kept coming, up the shaft, and gored his horse, and threw
him. Paun's horse would have none of it and carried him straight past. Caradoc lost his spear getting down,
and faced it with a dagger.

Then the horns, and the Wolves. Sir Rayne put a spear into the boar to make sure of it. Sir Hywel, who has
the best hands among them for wounds, bound up Sineda, and brought him back from very near death.

Sir Rayne told Lothwellen that his companion was nearly dead, his horse was dead, and he would be walking
home. Lord Clefford told Sir Rayne he was too hard on the boy: that had been a damned good boast, and it was
a fine dinner. Sir Elad said the answer was both. This was not the quarry they had come for, he said, and
they had made the best of it.

The boys asked for the tusks. The knights gave one to Sineda, who had struck first, and one to Lothwellen,
who had struck last. They brought Sineda home in a peasant's cart, and the surgeon at Sarum said that he
might live and he might not. Riding back, the boys heard one of the knights say that at least they would
never be bored with this lot.
