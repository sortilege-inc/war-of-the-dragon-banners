---
title: The Choosing
part: Sarum and Glastonbury, 462 · played 9 January 2026
---

Drust was over his flux and working in the stables when a servant came for him: the Wolves were back and
needed help with a cart. They had not left with a cart. In it were the butchered boars, a sour-faced Sir
Moriad nursing his leg, and Sineda, pale as a corpse, with his neck and shoulder bound.

The boys carried Sineda to the chapel in the fortress, to the monk who keeps the healing arts there, while
Paun ran ahead to find him. The monk was a round man in brown, and when he saw the boy he turned on the
knights. He has not had fifteen winters, he said. You took him boar hunting? Did you mean to get him killed?
Sir Rayne gave him a look, and he stopped and got to work. Sir Rayne did not look at the altar.

The monk cleaned and stitched the wounds while Drust and Lothwellen held Sineda down, packed them with
herbs and honey, and bound them again. Then he asked whose squire the boy was. Sir Rayne said that had not
been decided. Sir Elad came in and said: I claim him. The monk took Sir Elad outside to tell him how bad it
was, and the boys did not manage to hear.

## Jenny

The serving girl found Drust and Lothwellen in the passage. Her name was Jenny, and she wanted to know
what had happened to the little one. It was only a scratch, they told her, shaking their heads. They
fetched Sineda's better shirt from his things, the one he had worn at the feast, and she took it to him with
a blanket and food from the kitchens, and fed him ale from a spoon. Sineda's pride did not let that go on
for long. He sent her away, and she said she would be back when she could.

## At the high table

That night the Wolves sat at the high table long after the food had gone cold, talking low. The boys served
them and listened. Drust heard Sir Eamon say they could take Sineda to Glastonbury, where the witches would
know what to do for him, and Sir Moriad answer that the boy needed clean Christian treatment, not the tender
mercies of pagan witches. Lothwellen put it together: they meant a pilgrimage, to pray for a miracle.

When the hall had emptied, Lothwellen went up to clear the plates and asked, for all of them, whether they
might go too. The Wolves looked at him for a long moment, and then Sir Rayne told him to fetch the others and
sit down.

They could stay and let God decide, the knights said. Or they could go to Londinium, where the Chapel of St
Peter would have the finest of everything, surgeons included, at great cost and a long road. Or they could
go the other way, to Glastonbury Abbey, whose waters are said to heal and whose white monks are known for
tending the sick. That road is shorter, but it runs into Summerland, the land of King Cadwy, who some say is
a witch king over a hundred years old, and into the country of the fair folk, who do not always welcome men
on their roads. The boys thought Londinium was too far for a boy so near death, and said so. The knights had
been leaning that way themselves. Sir Moriad said they should go as pilgrims, crosses and all. Sir Rayne
said he would not wear the sign of the nailed god, but he would go. Lord Clefford warned that a straight road
in Summerland is seldom as short as it looks. Sir Rayne said: do not eat their food.

Then the knights questioned them.

Lord Clefford asked what it is to be a knight. Paun said: to serve your lord, defend his lands, protect those
who need it, and be what young men look up to. Lothwellen said it was to hold to noble virtues, champion those
who cannot defend themselves, fight when fighting must be done, and show a better way. Drust said he meant to
hold a straight line in a crooked world, and to keep his word when it would be easier to let it go: a candle
carried in a storm, foolish perhaps, but beautiful. We knew we liked you, said the knights.

Sir Eamon put his favourite riddle: you can save your mother, your wife or your child, and only one. Paun
said there is no tomorrow without the next generation, and chose the child. Lothwellen chose the child too,
because his mother and his wife would never forgive him otherwise. Drust chose his wife: to save his mother
would be piety and his child duty, but to save his wife would be selfish, and sometimes a man has to be
selfish to stay warm in a cold world. Lord Clefford said that was his own answer.

Sir Rayne asked whether the gods had spun each man's fate long ago, or whether he wove it himself.
Lothwellen said he wove it, and that the gods themselves can be surprised by what men choose. Drust said the
same, because to believe his fate already chosen would be to believe himself important, and he was a thread,
a loose one perhaps. Paun, the Christian, said that God had probably chosen his path, but had not told him,
and he would have to find it out. When Sir Rayne marvelled at a god so sparing with his words, Paun said his
god had a wider range of responsibilities than any one of Sir Rayne's, and he was not that important.

Sir Hywel asked what they most hoped to learn from the Wolves. Lothwellen said: to be a good man, and to
stay one; and then, as an afterthought, how to kill a boar before it kills you. Drust said: to protect those
who cannot protect themselves, by sword, by song, or by war. Paun said he wanted to learn the ease they had
with great trouble, and with each other. Sir Moriad asked why men swear oaths. Drust said that the world is
chaos, and an oath is a man writing the future before it happens. Sir Eamon asked what they would have the
bards sing of them in a hundred years. Paun said: that he had driven back the Saxons. Sir Moriad slammed his
hand on the table, and all of them spat to one side. Drust said he did not want to be a statue, which the
rain wears away, but a song, which travels from mouth to mouth. Sir Rayne said the heathen Saxons have a
saying he rather liked: cattle die, kinsmen die, a man himself dies, but the fame of his deeds never dies.

Sir Elad said that the Wolves were a family, not all of them by blood, and worked as one, and that the boys
seemed to understand that already. They would take Sineda to Glastonbury. At Sineda's bedside Sir Elad
told him: you will be my squire, and I expect you to live through this.

## The choosing

Later that night Paun, still in the hall, saw the knights settle the rest. There had been no squire among
them that more than one knight did not want, and in the end they drew lots and threw dice for the order of
choosing. He slipped out before he was seen, and told Drust he was in trouble: it looked like Lord
Clefford.

In the morning Sir Rayne found Lothwellen keeping watch outside Sineda's door, told him he was proudest of
him so far, because he already understood that a knight must first be a good man, and claimed him.

Lord Clefford sent Drust to the market for good linen in colours that suited him, and to Lady Trisha to be
measured: his squire would be properly dressed. He asked whether Drust could read, and when Drust said he
had always wanted to learn, said they would see to it. He told Drust to be at mass that evening, and to keep
an ear open for stories.

Paun was stuffing straw targets when he felt a point at his back, and swung blind with the target, and
missed, and was tripped. It was Sir Eamon. The lesson was awareness. Sir Eamon had won the draw, he said, and
chosen Paun, because Paun seemed to understand that a knight's greatest strength is his mind and not his
sword arm. He was a household knight without land, and Paun would share his wages; he would do his best
by him.

## Glastonbury

The road through Summerland to Glastonbury was, in the end, uneventful. The fair folk let them be. At the
abbey Sir Rayne made his prayers for Sineda somewhere else. Sineda prayed for a miracle, and was bathed in
water drawn from the sacred spring, and healed three times as fast as a man should, and came out of it with
no lasting harm.
