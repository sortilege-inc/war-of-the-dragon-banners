It took some weeks of healing. Sir Perrin had been struck on the head with a giant's club, and might never
be quite right again. Sir Elad would not let Roderick out of his sight: the boy was their lord, and he was
put with Sir Hywel, who had no squire of his own to mind, though in practice he trailed after Sineda.

Then the Wolves did what Merlin wanted. They rode north out of Cameliard into the wild country where
Cambria, Cumbria and Logres meet, a great forest with few roads through it, and then none. When the road
ran out the knights asked the way, and Merlin pointed north, towards the mountains, where three kingdoms
meet.

## The white hart

On a hill above the forest stood a white hart, looking down at them. Sir Rayne said it was a sign: his god
had sent one of his own to show them the way. And he spurred after it, and the other knights after him, and
Merlin smirked and said, come along, there are adventures afoot. White beasts are signs of purity and good
fortune, and to kill one is close to murdering an innocent. Paun shouted after Sir Rayne that it was not to
be hunted, which was not quite what he meant.

The knights' chargers left the squires' rounceys behind in the trees. Paun kept thinking he saw things at
the edge of his sight and heard things at the edge of his hearing, and kept jerking his horse's head round.
Paun and Sineda stayed together, and then Sineda's horse went into a ditch neither of them had seen. It was
not badly hurt, but it took saplings for levers, and rope, and a long time, to get it out, and when they had,
they were alone in a quiet, misty forest with no idea where anyone was.

They tried moss, which grows on one side of a tree, and following their own tracks back, and one idea after
another, each worse than the last. Then they heard laughter and bells in the distance. Sineda wanted to
go the other way, and he found the other way, fast. Paun saw a path lit by dappled sunlight leading away, and
the white hart standing in it, on a rock above running water, waiting for them. Sineda said that someone who
knew more about these things than he did had said to follow it, so they followed it. It was no longer running
from them. It was leading them.

## The man in the clearing

It led them along the river to a sunlit clearing, where a man was building himself a house of fallen timber.
He greeted them as lords. They told him they were lost and looking for the road. There are no roads here, he
said. This was the great forest between the lands, which some call the Forest Perilous: to the north,
Cumbria and the lands of the Picts; to the south, Logres, and all those who still think they are Romans; to
the west, those who have never forgotten who they are. This was the middle. Few came here except by choice.

He asked them a great many questions.

Why did they want to be knights? Paun said a knight is someone responsible and capable, whom people trust and
ask for help, and he wanted to be that. Sineda said he did it for his family. His father had been knighted on
the battlefield; Picts are not known for knightly virtues; his family had been given a chance that few like
them get, and he could not waste it. He was not fond of the part where people try to kill him with pointed
sticks.

What makes a good knight? Sineda said: strength, winning, bringing your enemies to heel, so that the strong
do not prey on the weak, because there is always someone bigger to put them back in line. Paun said that he
saw two kinds of knight in the stories, one hard to tell from a bandit and the other hard to tell from a king,
and he would sooner be the second, and see fewer of the first. Paun asked Sineda whether that was what they had
done to the giants. Giants do not count, said Sineda; they are not people, and they hung our lord up like meat. The man asked whether
it was only who made the rules that mattered, or what the rules were.

Why have laws at all? Paun said that when he began as a squire he knew nothing and had to be taught, and laws
were how you teach people when you cannot stand beside them the whole time. His example of a good law was the
divine law against coveting another man's wife, and he thought of a certain bedchamber at Carohaise as he said
it. He said you can tell an honest man because his deeds match his words, and he would always rather work with
that man. Asked whether he would drown himself in a river at his lord's order without knowing why, Sineda said he
would. Paun said that a squire starts from the presumption that his knight has a good reason and means no
needless harm, even when he is not told it. You are a very trusting young man, said the man.

He asked Sineda how someone so small meant to impose his will on anyone. Sineda said he hoped to get stronger
with training, and in any case it was not for himself: he would find a good wife and have children taller and
stronger than he was, and the family would climb. He asked what they sought, and they told him: a banner. They
did not know why a banner mattered; Merlin did. What they had been told, and what the dreams said, was that it
would help them find the man who would challenge Vortigern, the king who had broken his oaths first by giving
away his subjects' land, and then murdered those who resisted.

He said he knew nothing of a banner. But there was an old Roman fort up in the mountains, abandoned since their
grandfathers' grandfathers' time, on a rocky promontory above the forest: follow the river north until the trees
break, then climb. If they were looking for an old banner they might find one there. Be ready, he said. Things
are not always as they seem.

As they rode away, Paun asked Sineda whether he had understood any of that. Sineda thought the man had just been
lonely and glad of someone to talk to.

## The fort

Sineda found tracks of cloven hooves going up beside the river. The clouds came in, and as night fell at the edge
of the trees the sky opened and they could see nothing but by lightning. By its flashes they saw a fortress on
the hill, which did not look ancient at all: every crenellation sharp, one tall tower, and no banner anywhere.
Fresh tracks went up the path, heavy horses, a day old at most.

There was a drawbridge, which they had seen only once before, at Sarum. It was down. The gate was shut, with no
knocker. Paun hailed the castle and got nothing back but thunder. Then he knocked, and after a moment the gate
swung open by itself. There were stables inside, with water in the barrels from the rain. When they looked
back, the gate was shut again, though neither remembered closing it.

In the courtyard, in a walled garden, a tall plinth carried a winged statue of Victory, of the old Roman kind,
and round its foot stood larger-than-life statues of armoured men, on guard. There was writing on the plinth
that neither of them could read.

Paun nearly fell over a shield broken almost in two, as if by some enormous blow. It was Sir Elad's. Then
broken armour, and a helmet caved in that he would have sworn was Sir Moriad's. Sineda, running through the
castle shouting for Sir Elad, tripped over a sword so big he had only ever seen one man use it: Sir Rayne's,
broken in half. Its blade was rusted nearly through, though Lothwellen, whose charge it was, would never have
allowed a spot of rust on it. Then a piece of Lothwellen's sword, broken off at the hilt. Nothing else. No bodies. No doors, except to
the stables and the gatehouse. The towers had no entrances at ground level. The great tower had steps going up
to a blank wall where a door should have been.

Hanging just out of reach above those steps was an old legionary's shield, the long curved kind. Sineda struck
it like a gong with the pommel of his sheathed sword. The sound went all round the garden, and the eight statues stepped down
from their pedestals, twice the size of men, and came on. One had angel's wings, and three words cut across its
breastplate.

Paun set himself with his great mace at the top of the steps, where there was room for two to stand, and shouted
at them: what have you done to Sir Elad and Sir Moriad? They did not answer. He waited until they came within
reach and struck the winged one, and it was like striking stone. Its blade came down on his shoulder and drove
him back.

Behind him a door had appeared in the blank wall, and Sineda, whose courage had left him, was at it, pulling and
pushing, and it would not open for him. A voice spoke some words in Latin, which he did not understand. Paun,
backing up to him, took hold of the door and pulled, and it opened, and there was light beyond it. Sineda was not
beside him. From where Paun stood, he had gone through. He went in after him, calling his name.

Sineda was still outside, alone with the statues. He dodged the first great sword, which struck the door instead
and rang off it as if the door were iron, and hit back with his quarterstaff, and it was like hitting stone. The
second one knocked him senseless.
